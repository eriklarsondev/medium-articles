---
title: "Dear AI Doomers, Have You Actually Built Software?"
createdAt: 2026-10-30
---

I hear it daily. Software engineers, coders, developers — whatever you want to call us — apparently, our profession has been rendered obsolete in the age of artificial intelligence. Every time companies like Anthropic and OpenAI release their latest models, the doomers scattered across social media are quick to announce that software engineers are cooked.

As a lead software engineer, I've spent nearly fifteen years working with early-stage startups and digital agencies, leading engineering teams, building SaaS products, and developing open-source tooling. And I think we've collectively lost the plot when it comes to managing expectations around what AI actually means for our profession.

First, take a deep breath. Software engineers are not obsolete. Far from it.

AI is undoubtedly a disruptive force, and not necessarily in entirely positive ways. Turn on the news or scroll through social media, and you'll find another headline about layoffs, shrinking engineering teams, and an increasingly difficult entry-level job market for junior developers.

I'll admit, it's a little disheartening. When your career is constantly being framed as something on the verge of extinction, it's difficult not to feel the weight of that narrative. The negativity is practically unavoidable.

When I scroll through social media, I've noticed a common trait among the folks black-pilling their audiences: they seem to lack a fundamental understanding of what software engineering actually entails.

Somewhere along the way, we've conflated the ability to generate code with the ability to engineer software. They're not the same thing.

I think there's another psychological element at play here, and it brings to mind the Dunning–Kruger effect.

AI has made it possible for people with little or no software engineering experience to generate applications that would have required considerably more knowledge and effort just a few years ago. That's genuinely impressive. But there's a difference between being able to produce a working application with AI and having the experience to understand the engineering decisions behind it.

When you haven't spent years debugging production systems, managing technical debt, navigating architectural tradeoffs, or dealing with the consequences of decisions that seemed perfectly reasonable at the time, you may not recognize the complexity you're overlooking.

And that's where I think some of the more extreme AI doomering comes from. People discover that they can prompt an AI agent to build something functional, and they extrapolate that experience into sweeping conclusions about the future of an entire profession. They mistake their newfound ability to produce software for a comprehensive understanding of what software engineering entails.

The irony is that the less experience you have with the discipline, the easier it can be to underestimate everything you haven't encountered yet.

Building an application that works today is an accomplishment. Understanding how to keep it working, secure, maintainable, and scalable as requirements change is an entirely different challenge.

The ability to use a tool effectively doesn't automatically make you an authority on the profession that tool is transforming.

Don't get me wrong. AI has been revolutionary in how we approach technical problems and implement solutions. I've been using AI-assisted coding for several years now, and I have to say, the improvements have been remarkable. The capabilities of these tools have grown exponentially.

Creating an entire REST API, building a new landing page, writing tests, or tackling countless other development tasks can now be accomplished in minutes. Things that once required hours of implementation can sometimes be completed in a fraction of the time.

As a software engineering lead, AI has drastically improved my workflow and overall efficiency. I use these tools every day. But that's not the whole story.

As a team lead, I'm accountable for the technical decisions being made. I need to understand why one approach is better than another, what we're gaining, and, more importantly, what we're sacrificing.

Software engineering is fundamentally about tradeoffs. Every system operates within constraints, whether that's budget, infrastructure, performance, scalability, security, or time. There's rarely a universally correct solution. There are decisions to be made, competing priorities to balance, and consequences that need to be understood.

AI can help explore those decisions and even recommend solutions, but someone still needs to understand the context in which those recommendations are being made.

Infrastructure, CI/CD pipelines, deployment strategies, and even complex system architectures can all be designed and implemented with the assistance of AI. But someone still needs to own those decisions. Someone needs to understand why a particular approach was chosen, what tradeoffs were made, and what happens when things inevitably go wrong.

And when they do go wrong, someone needs to be accountable. We can't just shrug our shoulders and say, "The AI messed up." That's not an acceptable answer when you're responsible for building and maintaining software that people and businesses depend on. We need to understand what happened, why it happened, and what needs to change to prevent it from happening again. AI can be part of the process, but it doesn't absolve us of responsibility for the outcome.

This is the meat of software engineering. Code generation is just one part of a much larger discipline, and AI has made that part considerably cheaper. But reducing the cost of producing code doesn't eliminate the complexity of engineering the systems that code ultimately becomes part of.

Prototyping a new product is one thing. AI can do that remarkably well. But evolving an established software product across multiple developers and engineering teams, maintaining architectural consistency, managing technical debt, and ensuring scalability and security is an entirely different challenge.

This is where experienced software engineers earn their keep. It's not just about getting something working. It's about making sure what you're building can continue to work, grow, and evolve without becoming a liability.

When I began my career in the early 2010s, learning to code was already difficult. Mix in cloud infrastructure, deployment pipelines, version control, database management, and the ever-growing ecosystem of frameworks and tooling, and there was an enormous amount to learn just to become a competent developer.

Today's aspiring developers have an entirely different landscape in front of them. AI can help bridge many of those learning gaps, explain unfamiliar concepts, and accelerate implementation in ways that simply weren't available when I started.

That's an incredible advantage. But I do wonder whether we're at risk of confusing the ability to produce working software with actually understanding how it works.

This is the part that genuinely concerns me.

I've recently seen companies move away from traditional technical assessments because candidates can now complete them using AI-assisted coding tools. I've also seen hiring managers question whether candidates even need to understand data structures and algorithms when those responsibilities can supposedly be delegated to an AI agent.

I think we're heading down a dangerous path if that's genuinely becoming the expectation.

Understanding data structures, algorithms, computational complexity, and the fundamentals of programming isn't about memorizing solutions to interview questions. It's about developing the ability to reason through technical problems, understand what's happening beneath the abstraction, and recognize when a proposed solution is inefficient or fundamentally flawed.

AI can generate an implementation, but if you lack the foundational knowledge to evaluate that implementation, how do you know whether you're making the right decision?

There's another thing I think we're overlooking: where does this leave the next generation of developers?

You can't expect to have experienced engineers without first having junior developers. Every senior engineer started somewhere. We learned by writing code, making mistakes, debugging problems, working alongside more experienced developers, and gradually taking on greater responsibility.

If companies increasingly rely on AI to handle the work traditionally assigned to junior developers, and simultaneously decide that foundational programming knowledge is no longer a hiring requirement, what exactly does the path into this profession look like?

You can't automate away the entry point to a career and expect the talent pipeline to take care of itself. Today's junior developers are tomorrow's senior engineers, architects, and technical leads. If we stop investing in their development, we're creating a problem that could take years to fully materialize.

And then there's the question of what happens when we start handing more and more responsibility over to AI agents.

We're already seeing agents take on increasingly complex tasks with little to no human oversight, even handling things like code reviews. I wouldn't be surprised to see an increase in adverse outcomes as companies become more comfortable letting these tools make decisions without someone properly validating the results.

There's a difference between using AI to accelerate engineering work and allowing it to make decisions that nobody on the team fully understands or independently validates. Poor architectural decisions, overlooked edge cases, security vulnerabilities, and accumulating technical debt can all become downstream consequences.

What looks like a productivity gain today can become a significant maintenance burden tomorrow.

We're so focused on how quickly AI can produce code that we're overlooking the long-term cost of maintaining it. At some point, the code itself becomes the liability.

As a team lead, that's something I take seriously. I'm invested in the long-term success of both my team and the products we're building. That means thinking beyond the immediate task or the next release. It means making decisions that allow the team to grow, the architecture to evolve, and the product to remain maintainable.

So, to the AI doomers predicting the end of software engineering: I have a question.

Have you actually built software?

Not just prompted an AI agent to create an application. Not just watched it generate a REST API or put together a polished frontend. Have you dealt with production deployments, broken integrations, changing requirements, architectural decisions, technical debt, and the responsibility of keeping a product running?

Because there's a pretty significant difference between watching an AI generate code and understanding what it actually takes to build and maintain software.

I've spent nearly fifteen years in this industry, and I've watched technologies, frameworks, languages, and development practices come and go. AI is arguably one of the most significant shifts I've seen in my career. I'm not dismissing it, and I'm certainly not suggesting we should ignore its capabilities. Quite the opposite. We should embrace these tools, learn how to use them effectively, and adapt.

But let's stop confusing the automation of programming tasks with the obsolescence of software engineers.

The barrier to building software is lowering. The responsibility of building good software isn't.
